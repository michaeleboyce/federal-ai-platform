The Mandate Moment in Federal Generative AI

The White House’s recent AI Action Plan includes a remarkable provision on technology adoption in the federal government.  It mandates that “all Federal agencies ensure—to the maximum extent practicable—that all employees whose work could benefit from access to frontier language models have access to, and appropriate training for, such tools. [emphasis added].” In the course of usual watered-down policy-speak, this kind of clear, unqualified directive is unusual.

The Action Plan appropriately recognizes that AI tools are becoming a critical foundation of the modern workplace, and that federal employees should have access to them. Yet while the mandate is universal, actually realizing it—especially for generative AI chatbots and coding agents that already have widespread market adoption—remains a challenge. Agencies already have the legal and technical tools they need to adopt frontier models safely; the central question now is how to use those tools so that every employee whose work could benefit has access. This piece sets out concrete steps for doing so and explains why the usual reasons to delay adoption should no longer be accepted as justification for withholding these systems from federal workers.

This mandate could not be more timely. On the order of a billion people worldwide now use LLM chatbots like ChatGPT in a typical week, and roughly 1–1.3 billion in a typical month. Similar studies from Pew in June 2025 found that thirty-one percent of U.S. adults use generative AI tools several times per day, and 63% use it at least several times per week. Far from the naysayers who describe it as hype without practical use cases, generative AI use is now comparable to other “fads” like a daily cup of coffee.

Critics raise familiar societal objections— effects on learning, privacy, surveillance, workforce impacts, and even skepticism that LLMs are useful at all. Advocacy communities have called for many of these systems to be shut down. This piece does not engage those debates. It takes the new mandate as a given and focuses on the practical question in front of policymakers for adoption. For the core commercial AI systems that have already found traction—chatbots and coding agents—this piece starts from the premise that federal employees are trusted professionals who can choose to use these tools competently and appropriately.

There is also a broader and more urgent reason why ensuring adoption matters. There is now a bipartisan consensus that this technology will be transformational. Both the Biden and Trump administrations referred to AI as “one of the most powerful technologies of our time” and an “industrial revolution” respectively. If that is true, it is critical that policymakers at all levels of government understand what these tools can and cannot do in practice, because they will be responsible for the policy, regulatory, procurement, and adoption decisions that shape how AI is used. AI is not a technology that can be managed in the abstract.

AI is not a technology that can be managed in the abstract

A classic article in the state capacity literature is Stephen Smith's New York Times essay on how the American elevator—overregulated, expensive, and captured by entrenched interests—helps explain why housing costs have skyrocketed. The problem, Smith argued, was not that we lack the technology but that we have made deploying it unreasonably difficult.

When it comes to AI in government, the same applies. We would not trust an agency to write elevator regulations if it refused to install elevators in its own buildings. Yet, on AI, we are still too often governed by the elevatorless.

Generative AI Adoption in the Federal Government

Before turning to specific steps agencies and lawmakers can take to realize the Action Plan's mandate, it is worth asking: how far has the federal government actually come in adopting generative AI tools and what are the results? At the agencies that have adopted these tools aggressively, the results have been staggering.

Chatbots have shown meaningful productivity gains

At Veterans Affairs, employees using generative AI tools save 2-3 hours per week, and over 70 percent report increased efficiency. Similar results emerged at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, one of the first agencies to deploy an internal generative AI chatbot to all staff. The CDC estimates its tools have saved over 41,000 hours and $3.7 million in labor costs, representing a 527 percent return on investment. The Department of Defense has also released numerous chatbots, including its recent GenAI.mil platform. Some of these have seen large successes– while vendor-reported, the generative AI platform AskSage at DOD reportedly reduced time spent on Authority to Operate documentation by approximately 95 percent.6

Yet generative AI adoption remains uneven

Yet adoption remains inconsistent. DHS rolled out its internal DHSChat tool to headquarters staff in late 2024 but then revoked policies permitting commercial tools like ChatGPT, pivoting entirely to in-house platforms with more limited capabilities. GSA's USAi platform and $1 OneGov offerings have generated attention. While these are important efforts, and GSA reports it is “actively partnering” with 15 agencies to deploy USAi, only HHS has publicly deployed them at scale—rolling out ChatGPT department-wide in September 2025 and Claude in December.

Where adoption has fallen flattest is coding agents—the other major product category seeing explosive enterprise growth. GitHub Copilot has reached 20 million users and is used by 90 percent of Fortune 100 companies. Stack Overflow's 2025 survey found that 84 percent of developers now use AI tools, with 69 percent of those using agents reporting increased productivity.  Yet only three agencies—VA, NIH, and CBP—have publicly adopted coding agent-specific solutions. At VA, where 2,000 developers use GitHub Copilot, CTO Charles Worthington testified the tools save approximately 8 hours per week.

These statistics likely undercount adoption. Many tools are bundled into existing Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace contracts without major announcements. But that is precisely why transparency matters: if agencies have adopted these tools, publicizing that fact makes it harder for holdouts to claim there is no precedent.

Guardrails Are Increasingly Straightforward

The current state, then, is that the AI Action Plan’s mandate of access to all federal staff  “whose work could benefit from frontier language models” is still a ways off.  A recent GAO report gave useful perspective into why federal AI tools adoption has been mixed. Results from interviews with officials at 10 of 12 agencies found that “existing federal AI policy may not account for or could present obstacles to the adoption of generative AI” and pointing specifically to concerns around cybersecurity, data privacy, and acquiring IT systems. These concerns are as much about perception as real policy blockers. The guardrails needed for commercial chatbots and coding assistants are simpler than agencies assume—or already in place.

Data Privacy Has A Clear Path in Government

The first of these arguments—and the one that comes up most consistently—is data privacy. When it comes to using AI systems in enterprise environments, there is almost an emotional element to it: the coverage of massive data centers and multimillion-dollar training runs, combined with the very real perception that when you use ChatGPT, your conversations are being used as training data, are felt as much as they are reasoned about. In conferences, blog posts, and thought-leader posts, the question "what happens to the data?" is often asked as a rhetorical flourish rather than a genuine inquiry seeking answers.

Putting the emotion aside,  the argument that AI companies use user data in ways that are inappropriate for sensitive government data is logical, but missing a critical point: this is not an AI challenge; it is a cloud computing challenge. And one the federal government solved for over a decade. Every time you use a cloud system, whether Zoom, Microsoft Office 365 or Google search, potentially sensitive data goes to an external server. That data can be used as training data—and in the case of standard Google search, often is.

But in the government context, there is a long-established process for addressing this scenario and authorizing cloud tools at the federal level: the Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program for civilian agencies, and CMMC, IL4/IL5, and similar standards on the defense side. These frameworks establish baselines for application security, data protection, and other measures to ensure federal data does not leak. Their core principles are also straightforward: it is about ensuring that you know your data boundary, who you are sending your data to, and what guardrails are in place (everything Including training, physical security in the case of infrastructure, data access, encryption, and authentication). If you send the data outside of your boundary, then those additional providers must also show they’ve implemented commensurate standards, and received third party auditing.

While no system is perfect, these frameworks have matured to the point where Congress has codified their adequacy. The FedRAMP Authorization Act of 2022 explicitly states that "the assessment of security controls and materials within the authorization package for a FedRAMP authorization shall be presumed adequate for use in an agency authorization to operate cloud computing products and services." This presumption was written into law precisely to combat the endless second-guessing and unspecified risk aversion that sometimes characterizes federal IT decisions.

There are an increasing number of generative AI systems that are FedRAMP authorized. The FedRAMP system operates using tiers established by NIST—low, moderate, and high—with high systems capable of handling the most sensitive data. As the chart shows, numerous systems from OpenAI and Anthropic’s models to Microsoft Copilot and Google Workspace offerings have achieved FedRAMP high. The most famous of these tools—the full ChatGPT enterprise—is actually not yet FedRAMP’ed; it is in-progress but will likely receive FedRAMP authorization soon. Another version, ChatGPT Gov, which is a more limited version designed to deploy entirely within an already authorized FedRAMP High environment, is available for government.

Indeed, advocating caution over data privacy concerns almost certainly enables the opposite. The Google Search bar is an actual AI interface—try asking it to "write a sonnet about cybersecurity"—and no agency blocks Google search. Agencies without approved, secured AI tools are leading staff to use shadow systems where data is exfiltrated to environments that are subject to training. All agencies need to do to protect their data is actually adopt secure enterprise AI tools that leverage the rigorous and established processes in place; the longer they wait, the more of their actual data will be exfiltrated.

Novel Cybersecurity Risks Are Narrower Than Portrayed

The GAO also found that cybersecurity concerns are preventing adoption at many agencies. While some risks specific to LLMs, are not present in traditional systems, what is notable about LLM-based systems is the near absence of almost any known, real-world cybersecurity exploit that take advantage of these new threat vectors.  In a world where over a billion people use these tools, it is striking how infrequently hackers have exploited vulnerabilities unique to LLMs.

Most public reports indicate that security incidents have come through the traditional components of AI systems: databases, caching layers, and user interfaces.. The ChatGPT Redis bug in March 2023—which exposed chat titles and payment information for 1.2 percent of ChatGPT Plus subscribers—was a software bug in session management, not an adversarial AI attack. These are real vulnerabilities, but they fall squarely within existing cybersecurity frameworks.

Even if we have not seen many of these attacks, are they possible? Two frequently discussed threats can be largely dismissed in the federal context. Jailbreaking involves tricking an LLM into ignoring its guardrails—for example, by instructing the model to "ignore all previous instructions" and then attempting to elicit prohibited outputs. But when federal employees are using logged, internal systems, jailbreaking is largely irrelevant: if an employee wanted to produce prohibited content, they could simply do so on a personal device or write it in Microsoft Word. One could argue it would actually be preferable if such attempts occurred on monitored government systems, where they could be detected and subject to discipline or removal. Data poisoning—corrupting a model's training data to introduce vulnerabilities—primarily threatens organizations that train their own models or acquire custom ones. Most civilian agencies can largely avoid this risk by leveraging commercial models that hundreds-of-millions of other people are using as well.

The more serious concern is prompt injection: hiding malicious prompts inside otherwise benign instructions, typically to cause the model to exfiltrate data or gain system access. Prompt injection is the highest-rated risk according to the Open Worldwide Application Security Project and is possible in internal government use cases, especially when chatbots or coding assistants connect to other systems. Researchers have demonstrated sophisticated attacks like the Microsoft 365 Copilot "EchoLeak" vulnerability, which showed that a crafted email could extract internal documents and API keys with zero user interaction. The Salesforce Agentforce "ForcedLeak" vulnerability demonstrated how indirect prompt injection could exfiltrate CRM data. But these vulnerabilities share two important characteristics: they were discovered by security researchers in testing scenarios, and they were patched before any confirmed malicious exploitation occurred.

The process of catching these vulnerabilities was also instructive: the fix to the Salesforce vulnerability did not involve fundamentally redesigning the LLM. Instead, Salesforce re-secured an expired domain that had remained on its Content Security Policy whitelist and enforced trusted URL allowlists to prevent output from being sent to untrusted endpoints. Or in other words, Salesforce made it so the core Salesforce systems were no longer allowed to send lots of user data to URLs it did not control. The vulnerability existed because of how the system connected to the internet, not only because of features in the underlying model.

AI providers are increasingly building these protections by default. In its GPT-5 system card, OpenAI noted that "to protect against exfiltration of sensitive data from connectors, after a call is made to a connector we switch browsing to only access cached copies of web pages. This prevents any further live network requests via browser where the sensitive contents are in context." Meanwhile, model-level defenses continue to improve. Claude Opus 4.5 demonstrates industry-leading robustness against prompt injection attacks, with an attack success rate of just 4.7 percent on strong adversarial tests—lower than even recent competing models like Gemini 3 Pro (12.5 percent), albeit on Anthropic’s own internal testing benchmarks.

The takeaway is that the guardrail against cybersecurity vulnerabilities is not dissimilar from the data privacy conversation: agencies should leverage existing security frameworks like FedRAMP, routinely patch when new vulnerabilities are found, and follow standard practices when connecting sensitive data to any system with internet access. The architecture of enterprise AI deployments—isolated from the open internet, subject to existing controls—is not novel. All agencies need to do to remain safe from AI-specific attacks is to follow existing federal cybersecurity policies.

Most Agencies Should Not Train Models

The above analysis builds on a  basic assumption that bears stating explicitly: most civilian agencies should not be training their own AI models. The data privacy and cybersecurity concerns that agencies cite—worries about training data exposure, model poisoning, proprietary information leakage—are largely concerns about model training, not model use. When an agency deploys a commercial chatbot or coding assistant through a FedRAMP-authorized platform, it is consuming a service, not building one. The model has already been trained; agency data is used to generate responses, not to update how the model works. OMB's April 2025 AI procurement memo already addresses this directly, requiring contracts to "permanently prohibit the use of non-public inputted agency data and outputted results to further train publicly or commercially available AI algorithms" without agency consent.

Beyond the policy question, there is also a practical one: agency-built models would almost certainly underperform commercial alternatives. In a widely cited 2019 essay, AI researcher Richard Sutton observed that the most important lesson from seventy years of AI research is that "general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin." Attempts to build domain-specific intelligence by encoding human expertise have repeatedly been overtaken by systems that simply scale computation and data. Agencies that invest in training custom models are swimming against this current—competing with frontier labs that spend billions on training runs and have access to vastly more data.

Removing barriers is not enough

The above discussed two of the major concerns the GAO identified as obstacles to adoption: data privacy and cybersecurity. On both, the conclusion is the same—existing frameworks are adequate, and the perceived barriers reflect misunderstanding more than genuine policy gaps. But the GAO's interviews surfaced other challenges that cannot be solved by pointing to existing authorities. Seven of twelve agencies "reported challenges related to having the funding needed to establish these resources and support desired generative AI initiatives." And on acquisition, "GSA, NASA, and VA officials reported experiencing delays in acquiring commercial generative AI products and services, including cloud-based services, because of the time needed to obtain Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program authorizations."

These are different problems. Budget constraints and authorization delays are not about agencies misunderstanding risk—they are about structural incentives and competing priorities. Solving them requires tools that go beyond clarifying guidance: transparency mechanisms that create accountability, budget processes that force prioritization, and authorization reforms that reduce friction. The following sections address each

Concrete Steps to Accelerate Adoption

Much of what needs to happen can be done by the executive branch alone—and at many agencies, it already is. The success stories at VA, DOD, and HHS demonstrate that agencies with motivated leadership and technically capable staff can navigate existing authorities to deploy these tools at scale. The challenge is the holdout agencies: those where competing priorities, risk-averse cultures, or simple inertia have kept adoption stalled despite the Action Plan's mandate.

For those agencies, momentum must come from outside their direct chain of command. This is where the White House’s Office of Management and Budget plays a critical role, using the budget process and reporting requirements to signal priorities and create accountability. Congress can amplify these efforts through oversight and legislation, though meaningful progress does not depend on it.

Addressing the remaining challenges the GAO identified—budget constraints and authorization delays—requires three types of action: transparency mechanisms that make laggards visible, budget processes that force prioritization, and authorization reforms that reduce friction.

Transparency as Pressure

While disclosure is often used as a tool used to prevent unintended consequences and increase consideration, not spur innovation. But the simplest tool to increase adoption can also be transparency: by turning the routine parts of oversight against risk taking and caution, the goal is to, as Sam Hammond puts it in his reaction to the AI Action Plan, “[flip] the burden of proof on agencies’ Chief AI Officers, requiring them to justify why an AI system wasn’t made accessible rather than the other way around.”

The Office of Management and Budget is uniquely positioned to enforce this shift. Under the E-Government Act of 2002, OMB's Office of the Federal Chief Information Officer has statutory authority to oversee federal IT policy, issue implementation guidance, and require agency reporting on technology adoption. This authority has been exercised repeatedly to drive compliance on everything from cybersecurity to cloud computing. And OMB's April 2025 AI memo, M-25-21, already established a framework for transparency on AI use: agencies must publicly post compliance plans, update AI use case inventories annually, and publicly report any waivers or determinations exempting high-impact AI systems from minimum risk management practices.

OMB should issue a new memorandum—following the model of the other recent AI memos —requiring agency Chief Information Officers to publicly certify compliance with the Action Plan's LLM mandate. The memo should define the basic tenets of what is sufficient to qualify, and reasonable categories for individuals who do not need access (a TSA officer who does not have a government-issued computer or phone, for example). In turn, the CIO’s certification should specify what general-purpose chatbot and coding assistant tools the agency provides and what percentage of the workforce has access. This data would fit naturally within the existing use case inventory required under the Advancing American AI Act, which already mandates that agencies maintain and publicly post inventories of their AI application. Agencies that cannot certify broad deployment should be required to request an exception—posted publicly on agency websites and subject to OMB review. The model already exists in M-25-21, which requires public reporting of waivers for high-impact AI use cases. A process as simple as public certification with a deadline would drive adoption in the remaining hold-outs while creating momentum for adoption.

Additionally, if Congress is interested, one fairly lightweight model here is the FITARA Scorecard. Since November 2015, the House Oversight Committee has released biannual scorecards grading 24 major agencies on IT management, developed in partnership with GAO. According to GAO, the share of agencies receiving a C or higher rose from 29 percent in 2015 to 100 percent by December 2021. When all 24 agencies achieved A grades in software licensing—a category added in 2017 when only two agencies had comprehensive licensing inventories—the category was retired. The same happened with data center consolidation. The scorecard does not carry any formal penalty. It simply makes performance visible, and consistent Congressional attention does the rest.

Adding a generative AI adoption category to the FITARA Scorecard would immediately create this dynamic. The category could track what percentage of agency staff have access to approved chatbot tools, whether coding agents are available to development teams, and whether the agency has posted any exceptions to broad deployment. None of this requires new legislation—only sustained attention from the relevant subcommittees working with OMB and GAO. The infrastructure already exists; it simply needs to be extended.

The Budget as Signal

If transparency creates pressure, the budget process creates prioritization. The goal is not necessarily new appropriations—the costs of these tools are modest, often in the single millions of dollars, and many agencies already have sufficient funds in management budget lines. New appropriations, of course, help, but simply requesting more money is nearly a tautology in policy recommendation circles. The more interesting question is how to use the levers of the budget cycle to signal priorities and ensure agencies allocate existing resources toward AI adoption.

The first lever is Spring Guidance—the annual memorandum OMB issues each spring that kicks off agency budget planning roughly eighteen months before a fiscal year begins. While Spring Guidance has not been publicly released since the Mulvaney-era memoranda of 2017, the process continues, and those familiar with it report that over time these documents have become something of a laundry list of administration priorities. But that is precisely why including something specific matters. A line directing agencies to show how they will fund chatbot and coding assistant deployment would raise eyebrows—and is actionable enough that a savvy budget officer would incorporate it into their planning.

Yet Spring Guidance alone is insufficient. Unless agencies are already motivated, they will try to avoid including items they think they can get away with omitting. This is where passback becomes critical. Passback is the stage where OMB reviews agency submissions and sends back its decisions, typically around the holidays, telling agencies what they can and cannot request. This is where priorities become line items.

There is a structural challenge to do this: OMB's budget offices are organized vertically—almost all budget examiners are assigned to specific agencies or subagencies, not cross-cutting functions. When it comes to IT, horizontal coordination is difficult. There is no single budget examiner responsible for ensuring every agency has funded AI tools. Driving adoption through the budget therefore requires making each individual examiner aware that this is a priority—and eventually putting a stake in the ground.

Passback is the moment to do this. The mechanism is usually not a formal memo but rather direction from a senior leader at OMB, such as one of the Principal Associate Directors, to their staff: ensure these budget line items are included in agencies' submissions. The language could be straightforward: "Agency will allocate resources for enterprise chatbot and coding assistant deployment, and will report to OMB on implementation progress and return on investment." Agencies receiving this direction would need to either comply or explain why they cannot—and that explanation would itself become a data point for oversight.

The recently leaked HHS passback for fiscal year 2026—a 64-page document dated April 10, 2025—provides a rare window into how specific this process can be, with line items for particular programs and offices. If similar specificity is applied to AI adoption, agencies will restructure their budgets accordingly—even before any new appropriations come online.

Accelerating Authorization

The final set of levers relates to the authorization process itself. Even when agencies want to adopt AI tools, the security authorization can create delays measured in months or years. But here too, existing authorities provide tools that have been underutilized. The Executive Branch can leverage them, or Congress can order them to, but both paths lead to the same result.

First, there is the question of the FedRAMP. There has been progress here: the FedRAMP 20x initiative, announced in March 2025, represents a genuine attempt to accelerate cloud authorization through automation. GSA has announced that AI-based cloud services providing conversational AI for federal workers will be prioritized for 20x authorization, responding to a request from the CIO Council. The first cohort of providers received FedRAMP 20x Low pilot authorizations in July 2025, and phase two launched in September. This is promising, but 20x is still ramping up and will take time to reach the scale needed to keep pace with the rapid iteration of AI products.

In the interim, the OMB Memorandum M-24-15 that defines the FedRAMP program provides a tool that can be expanded. The memorandum permits agencies to authorize "use of the covered product or service on a trial basis for a specified period of time, not to exceed twelve months, with the goal of more easily supporting a potential full FedRAMP authorization." This trial authority should be extended to twenty-four months for generative AI products, provided the agency accepts the risk. A two-year window would allow agencies to deploy current tools while keeping pace with subsequent versions—GPT-5 to GPT-6, Claude 4 to Claude 5—without restarting the authorization clock each time the underlying model improves.

But the deeper problem is not the FedRAMP process itself. It is what happens after a product receives FedRAMP authorization. Many agencies have already authorized cloud platforms—Microsoft Azure, AWS GovCloud, Google Cloud—that now include AI capabilities as standard features. These capabilities are covered by the existing authorization boundary. Yet agency CISOs frequently decline to enable them, citing unspecified concerns about AI or arguing that the AI features require separate authorization.

This pattern reflects a structural incentive problem. A CISO who enables a new feature takes on risk; a CISO who blocks it faces no consequences. A 2023 report based on interviews with more than twenty federal security practitioners documented the dynamic: when authorizing officials change, they often decline to approve what their predecessors approved, forcing systems back through review. And even for authorized systems, the incentive is to change as little as possible—enabling new features (like coding assistants in existing cloud packages) might trigger reauthorization, while leaving things alone maintains the status quo. The result is that AI capabilities sit dormant inside platforms agencies have already paid for and already authorized.

The FedRAMP Authorization Act provides a tool to address this. As noted earlier, the statute establishes that FedRAMP authorizations carry a "presumption of adequacy"—meaning agencies must presume that a FedRAMP-authorized product's security controls are sufficient for agency use at the authorized impact level. If an agency wants to impose additional requirements beyond the FedRAMP baseline, it must document a "demonstrable need" for those requirements. And if an agency finds an existing FedRAMP package "wholly or substantially deficient," the agency, usually acting through the CISO, must document the reasons.

This statutory language has not been adequately enforced. The implementing guidance should make clear that when a cloud platform has received FedRAMP authorization, and that authorization covers AI capabilities within the platform's boundary, agency CISOs cannot simply decline to enable those capabilities without documentation. First, OMB or Congress should require FedRAMP to provide a list of what FedRAMP services have AI capabilities, and which agencies have adopted those broader services, something FedRAMP already has  on its website. Then, acting through the CISO Council—which coordinates security policy across agencies—all CISOs should be required to review their existing ATOs for major cloud platforms and confirm whether AI capabilities are explicitly included. Where they are not, CISOs should be required to either update the ATO to include them or provide written justification for exclusion, submitted to OMB or Congress as part of mandate compliance reporting.

This is not a matter of forcing agencies to accept risk they are uncomfortable with. It is a matter of requiring agencies to articulate their risk concerns specifically, document them as the statute requires, and subject those determinations to review. The presumption of adequacy exists precisely to prevent vague, undocumented risk aversion from blocking adoption of tools the government has already determined are secure enough for federal use.

Conclusion: Innovation is a Choice

The AI Action Plan's LLM mandate is ambitious precisely because it requires something  that rarely succeeds in government: innovation. R. Kent Weaver's foundational work on the "politics of blame avoidance" documents why. Voters punish failures far more severely than they reward equivalent successes, so the rational bureaucrat prioritizes prevention over promotion—finding reasons why something cannot be done rather than ways it could be done. This asymmetry is reinforced by law. In Norton v. Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance, the Supreme Court held that courts cannot compel agencies to pursue broad programmatic goals—only "discrete" actions that agencies are specifically "required" to take. A mandate to "modernize" or "adopt AI" is precisely the kind of general directive that courts will not enforce. Prevention mandates have teeth; innovation mandates are aspirational.

Yet the mandate need not suffer this fate. Unlike directives to "be innovative," ensuring that federal employees have access to commercial chatbots and coding assistants do not require inventing new technology or reimagining how government works. The infrastructure already exists: FedRAMP provides a mature authorization framework with multiple frontier models already certified at the High level; OMB has budget levers that can signal priorities without new appropriations; congressional committees can add AI adoption to existing oversight mechanisms without passing new legislation. The ingredients are in place.

None of these levers is a silver bullet—anyone who has held a management or oversight role in government knows that no single policy creates action on its own. The agencies that have already succeeded demonstrate what is possible. But whether the rest follow will depend less on the policy itself than on whether the people and institutions responsible for it keep paying attention. Mandates do not implement themselves. What will make AI adoption real in the federal government is not the directive—it is sustained commitment from the leaders, oversight bodies, and career staff who choose, month after month, to care.
