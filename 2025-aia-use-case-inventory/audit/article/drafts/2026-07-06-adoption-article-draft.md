The White House’s Artificial Intelligence (AI) Action plan released last year was notable in its unabashed support for AI and large language model (LLM) adoption in the federal government.  It mandated that “all Federal agencies ensure—to the maximum extent practicable—that all employees whose work could benefit from access to frontier language models have access to, and appropriate training for, such tools. [emphasis added].” In the course of usual watered-down policy-speak, this kind of clear, unqualified directive is unusual.

Now with the Office of Management and Budget reporting its 2025 consolidated AI Use Case inventory, we have the perfect moment to see how far the federal government has come in realizing that mandate. It seems clear that this vision of substantial AI adoption has in many ways bore fruit. [statistics here]

The rate of this adoption is stunning–compared to other technology adoption patterns of technology adoption: whether the cloud of computers themselves, the roll-out of generative AI tools appears to have compressed a normally decade-long process into a couple of years. Yet, the use case inventory also identifies lingering gaps—whereas initial rollout of generative AI capabilities specifically has been substantial, the agentic coding and heavily integrated workflows that have risen since early 2024 are still notably missing.

From the perspective of the AI Action Plan’s mandate, the data shows that the question now shifts from “are agencies using these tools” to two related ones: How does the federal government start to cement and build on this adoption and what should its response be to the next wave of coding agents that promise to meaningfully  enhance state capacity? Exploring these topics means also discussing the role of use case inventories—what information do inventories provide us in the world where AI, and generative AI specifically, is as ubiquitous as spreadsheets and email?

Focusing on the adoption question, this piece will not add to the conversation of how or when the government should use generative AI—there are plenty of sophisticated pieces that cover every aspect of those issues. Instead, it will focus on adoption as an end in its own right. Whatever one’s opinion about the promise or peril of AI and LLM’s in government, there is a core intuition that is important to remember: As we consider how government will respond to and oversee these technologies on the societal level, it is important we are not led by the blind. If government staff—who will often write the rules and create the environment for how AI develops—cannot use the technology themselves, and especially in the workplace where much of the impact of AI will be felt, the outcomes of this transformation will be worse. This piece finds that while progress has been made in ensuring federal employees can access LLM tools, some critical steps should be taken to cement this progress and prepare them for the next step in AI adoption. Doing so will ensure not only a more effective government, but a government workforce that understands and is ready to meet the AI moment in society at large.

Taking the next steps

What does an adoption approach look like moving forward? Much of the groundwork is in place. Even with calls for additional law making, the policy foundation exists to further expand adoption. Congress has passed two laws: the AI in Government Act and the Advancing American AI Act, two successive administrations have upheld policies that set binding guardrails for AI use in government (OMB 24-10 and 25-21) that uphold the concept of certain mandatory practices for “high-risk” AI use cases, and the agencies themselves have published over [fill in here] pages of strategies and policies since 2023 specific to AI use.

So cementing the progress in adoption requires additional steps through the policy mechanisms that can serve as binding policy for agencies: law, executive order and OMB policies. Many of these recommendations should be read and understood that even when referring to OMB, the effect can be achieved by law, EO, or just direct OMB action. Indeed, much of the history of AI oversight in the government follows different overlapping actions where usually OMB, and sometimes some other agencies such as OSTP, DHS, etc. are ordered to take an action. For example, OMB’s original binding AI policy—M 24-10—was actually a legal requirement from the the 2020 AI in Government Act. The Biden Administration’s later “ordered” its release in their AI Executive Order.

Same too with the Federal AI Use Case Inventory, first appearing as a requirement in the Trump Administration’s 2020 AI Executive Order, continuing in the Biden administration, and then becoming codified into law through 2028 in the 2022 Advancing American AI Act. Unless otherwise stated, all of the following recommendations all are within OMB’s policy authority, but similarly requiring them by law or executive order would further emphasize and guarantee their effect.
